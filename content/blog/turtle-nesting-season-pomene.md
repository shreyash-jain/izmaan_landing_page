For about half the year the beach below the lodge is just a beach. Then, some time in October, the first set of tractor-wide tracks appears in the sand overnight — up from the water, a churned hollow at the top, and back down again. **Turtle nesting season in Pomene** has started, and it will run until March.

This is the guide to seeing it: which turtles come ashore, when they lay and when the eggs hatch, how to watch without doing harm, and how to plan a stay that actually gives you a chance of being there on the right night.

## Key takeaways

- **Season:** roughly **October to March**, with most nesting between **November and January**.
- **Hatchlings:** emerge about **two months after laying** — so late December through March.
- **Two nesters:** **loggerheads** (more often) and **leatherbacks** (rarer, enormous).
- **It happens at night**, usually on a rising or high tide, on dark, undeveloped sand.
- **No sighting is guaranteed.** Staying several nights in peak season is what changes the odds.
- **Light is the enemy.** No white torches, no flash, no phone screens on the beach.

## Why Pomene's dark beach is ideal for nesting turtles

Sea turtles are fussy about where they lay, and their requirements are the opposite of what most beach destinations offer: no lights behind the beach, no noise, no vehicles. A female that feels exposed will turn around and go back to the sea without laying at all — it's called a false crawl, and it happens constantly on developed coastlines.

Pomene passes the test almost by accident. It sits inside the **Pomene National Reserve**, at the end of a long sand track, with a small village, no town, no street lighting and no strip of resorts behind the dune. Walk the beach at 9pm and the brightest thing you'll see is the Milky Way.

That darkness isn't just atmosphere. It's the single reason hatchlings here can still find the sea — the part of the story we come back to at the end.

## Loggerhead vs leatherback: what nests here and when

Two species haul out on this coast, and they're easy to tell apart once you've seen either one.

**Loggerheads** are the ones you're most likely to meet: reddish-brown, hard-shelled, about a metre of shell and roughly a hundred kilograms, with the heavy blunt head that gives them the name. They're the workhorse nester of southern Mozambique, and most tracks you find in the morning belong to them.

**Leatherbacks** are the rare prize — the largest turtle on Earth, approaching two metres and several hundred kilograms, with a ridged, rubbery shell instead of a hard one. They come ashore in far smaller numbers, and seeing one is uncommon even for people who live on this coast.

Both nest at night, usually on a rising or high tide, when the walk up the beach is shortest. A female hauls out, digs a body pit, excavates an egg chamber with her back flippers, lays somewhere around a hundred eggs, covers and camouflages the nest, and drags herself back to the water. It takes an hour or two — and once she's laying she enters a kind of trance and is far less likely to abandon it.

![A loggerhead turtle nesting by moonlight at the top of the beach in Pomene, Mozambique, digging her nest in the dry sand — turtle nesting Pomene after dark](/images/blog/turtle-nesting-season-pomene-nesting-female.jpg)

**Green turtles** live over the reefs here year-round rather than nesting on the beach; those are the ones snorkellers meet, and they're covered in our [diving and snorkelling guide](/journal/diving-snorkelling-pomene).

## Month-by-month: nesting vs hatching

The two halves of the season overlap, which is worth understanding before you pick your dates.

| Month | Nesting (females ashore) | Hatching (hatchlings emerging) |
| --- | --- | --- |
| October | Season opens — first tracks | — |
| November | Building | — |
| **December** | **Peak** | First emergences late in the month |
| **January** | **Peak** | Building |
| February | Winding down | **Peak** |
| March | Last nests | **Peak**, tailing off |
| April | — | Occasional late emergences |

The mechanism is simple: eggs incubate in the warm sand for **about two months**, so whatever is laid in December hatches in February. A female also nests several times a season, returning every couple of weeks, which is why activity concentrates rather than spreading evenly.

## The best time within the season for a sighting

If you want one answer: **December and January**.

Those two months sit at the peak of nesting *and* catch the start of hatching, so you have two chances every night rather than one. They also coincide with warm water and the South African festive-season window, which means they're the first weeks to book out — more on that below.

If your priority is specifically **hatchlings**, come in **February or March**. Fewer females are coming ashore by then, but the beach is producing hatchlings, and watching a hundred of them scramble down the sand at dawn is the more emotional of the two experiences.

If you want to combine turtles with **humpback whales**, come in **October or early November**, when the last whales of the [whale-watching season](/journal/whale-watching-pomene-mozambique) are heading south past the point while the first turtles are hauling out at night. It's the only overlap in the calendar, and it's a good one.

## How to watch responsibly

This is the part that decides whether your night is harmless or damaging. The rules are short and non-negotiable.

- **No white light.** No torches, no headlamps, no flash photography, no phone screens pointed at the beach. If you need light to walk, use a **red filter** and keep it low and pointed at your feet.
- **Stay behind and stay low.** Approach a nesting female only from behind, crouched, and never get in front of her head. A turtle that sees you before she starts laying will abandon the nest.
- **Keep your distance and keep quiet.** Several metres back, no crowding, no talking above a murmur.
- **Never touch** a turtle, a nest, or a hatchling. Don't lift them, don't "help" them to the water, don't put them in a bucket.
- **Let hatchlings crawl.** That scramble down the beach is how they orient themselves and build the strength for their first swim. Carrying them to the sea skips something they need.
- **Leave the beach as you found it.** Fill in any holes you dug during the day and take furniture and gear off the sand at night — a hatchling that falls into a hole doesn't get out.
- **No vehicles on the beach**, ever, and especially not after dark.

## Combining turtle season with the rest of October–March

Turtle season lands squarely in Pomene's warm months. The nights are for the beach; the days fill themselves.

- **Warm-water diving and snorkelling** — the sea is at its warmest, and green turtles are on the reef year-round.
- **Fishing** — the game-fishing calendar runs strongly through the southern summer.
- **Kitesurfing** — steady wind over an empty lagoon.
- **The last whales** — October and early November only.
- **Doing nothing at all** — on a beach this empty, a legitimate itinerary.

## Why undeveloped, dark beaches matter

Every sea turtle species on this coast is listed as threatened or worse, and roughly **one hatchling in a thousand** survives to adulthood — a number that assumes it reaches the water at all.

Hatchlings find the sea by crawling toward the brightest, lowest horizon, which on a natural beach is starlight and moonlight on the ocean. Put a security light, a bar or a row of villas behind that beach and the brightest horizon is now inland. The hatchlings turn the wrong way and die of exhaustion or predation before dawn. It's one of the most avoidable ways a nesting beach is destroyed, and it happens quietly, one development at a time. Sand temperature during incubation also determines whether a clutch comes out male or female, so undisturbed dune sand matters nearly as much as the darkness.

Which is the honest argument for a place like Pomene. This beach still works as a nursery because nobody built a town on it — and staying somewhere that keeps its lights off is part of keeping it that way.
