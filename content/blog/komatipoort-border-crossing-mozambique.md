The drive from Johannesburg to Pomene is about 1,100 km, and roughly four minutes of it decide whether the trip happens. That is how long an immigration officer at Lebombo needs to look at your paperwork and either stamp it or send you home. Get the **Komatipoort border crossing** right and the rest is just distance.

This is the version nobody writes: what you actually need at the counter, what the insurance really costs, what the Mozambican police will look for on the EN1, and how the remaining 690 km to Pomene breaks up. It's written for the trip we see most often — a loaded 4×4, a family or two, coming through Komatipoort in December.

## Key takeaways

- **Lebombo (SA) and Ressano Garcia (Mozambique)** are the two halves of the same crossing. Normal hours: **06:00 to midnight**, extended over peak holidays.
- **South Africans need no visa** for stays under 30 days. Other passports differ — check the government portal, not a blog.
- **Third party insurance is compulsory**: around **R280** for a car, SUV or bakkie, valid **30 days** from entry.
- **A letter of authority** is non-negotiable if the vehicle is financed, rented or not registered in your name.
- **Two triangles, two yellow or green vests, a country sticker** — the kit the law expects, checked at roadblocks.
- **Komatipoort to Pomene is ~690 km.** Break it overnight. Never arrive in the sand after dark.

## The Komatipoort border crossing, explained

The names confuse people, so start here. **Komatipoort** is the South African town on the N4. The border post itself is **Lebombo**, a few kilometres east of town. Cross the line and the same facility becomes **Ressano Garcia** on the Mozambican side, where the N4 becomes the EN4 and runs about 100 km down to Maputo.

Standard operating hours are **06:00 to midnight, seven days a week**. Over Easter and from roughly mid-December to mid-January the authorities extend those hours, occasionally to 24-hour operation for travellers — but that is announced season by season, not guaranteed. The two governments have also been building a one-stop post here for years, which will eventually mean one set of counters instead of two. Until it is fully live, budget for both.

You may have read about 60-hour queues at this border. Those figures are **freight** queues — trucks waiting to clear cargo, sometimes backed up 7 to 15 km. Cars and buses are processed separately and the experience is nothing like it. On a normal weekday morning you can be through in under an hour. On the Friday before Christmas, plan for several.

The single best piece of timing advice: **cross mid-morning on a weekday**, roughly 09:30 to 13:30, when the early rush has cleared and the afternoon one hasn't started. Avoid the first weekend of the December holidays entirely if you have any flexibility, and if you don't, be in the queue by 06:00.

## Documents: the checklist that gets you through

Lay these out in a folder before you leave home. Not the cubbyhole — a folder.

**For every person:**

- **Passport**, valid for at least **three months beyond the date you exit** Mozambique if you hold a SADC passport, six months if you don't, with **three blank pages**. An emergency travel document will not do.
- **A visa or entry authorisation** if your nationality requires one — see the section below.

**For children under 18:**

- An **unabridged birth certificate** showing both parents.
- If one parent isn't travelling, an **affidavit of consent** from the absent parent, dated within the last three months. Sole-custody or guardianship documents where they apply.

This catches more families than anything else on the list, usually because someone assumed a school holiday trip wouldn't be checked. It is checked. If you're travelling as a group of two families, get every certificate copied and certified in the same week so nothing is out of date. Our guide to [family stays at Pomene](/journal/family-holiday-mozambique-pomene) covers the rest of the planning for a trip with children.

**For the vehicle:**

- The **original registration certificate** (the NaTIS document). Bring a printed copy too.
- A **certified letter of authority** if the vehicle is financed, leased, rented or registered in anyone else's name — including a spouse's. From the bank, the rental company or the owner, naming you, the vehicle and your travel dates.
- Your South African **cross-border cover confirmation** from your own insurer, if you carry comprehensive cover and want it to apply.
- The **temporary import permit (TIP)**, completed at the border. Small fee, paid in meticais. **Do not lose the stamped copy** — you hand it back on the way out.
- The compulsory **third party insurance certificate**, covered next.

## Third party insurance for Mozambique: what it costs

Mozambican law requires every foreign vehicle to carry local third party cover before it is allowed in. This is not optional and it is not the same as your South African policy.

The price is set by vehicle class, so shopping around achieves nothing. [Hollard Mozambique](https://www.hollard.co.mz/personal-insurance/travel/third-party-for-foreign-vehicles), one of the authorised issuers, lists **R280 for a car, SUV or bakkie**, R140 for a motorcycle and R700 for a commercial vehicle. Cover lasts **30 days from the day the vehicle enters** the country, with liability up to R1 million.

You have two options. Buy it **at the border** on the day — a counter on the Mozambican side, quick enough when it isn't peak season. Or buy it **in advance** from an AA travel shop, several garages along the N4, or outdoor retailers, and arrive with the certificate already printed. In December, buy it in advance. The half hour you save is worth more than the errand.

Two things people get wrong. A **trailer needs its own cover**, priced separately. And the 30 days runs from **entry**, not from purchase — so a certificate bought a fortnight early still gives you a full month once you cross.

## Visas and entry authorisation in 2026

If you hold a **South African passport**, this section is short: no visa for tourist stays of up to 30 days, as with most SADC citizens. Turn up with the passport and the paperwork above.

Everyone else needs to look it up properly, because Mozambique has changed its entry rules more than once since 2023. A group of visa-exempt nationalities — the UK, most of the EU, the US, Canada, Japan and others — can enter without a visa, but there is now an online pre-authorisation step for some travellers, applied for before departure rather than at the counter. Fees and processing times have moved around too.

We're not going to publish a number that expires. Check **[the Mozambican government's own portal, evisa.gov.mz](https://evisa.gov.mz/)**, against your specific passport, and do it when you book rather than the week you leave. If you're bringing overseas guests to join a South African road trip, this is the item to confirm first.

## The vehicle kit Mozambican law expects

Roadblocks on the EN1 are routine and mostly uneventful. What makes them uneventful is having the kit.

- **Two red warning triangles** that stand on their own, with sides no shorter than **425 mm**. **Towing? Four** — two for the vehicle, two for the trailer.
- **Two reflective vests** in **luminous yellow or green**. They must be in the cab, not the trailer, and worn if you stop at the roadside or at an accident.
- A **country-of-origin sticker** on the **right-hand (driver's side) rear**, at least 150 mm clear of the number plate.
- **Trailers** additionally need white reflectors facing forward, red reflectors facing rearward, and continuous amber reflective tape along the sides and back.
- A **serviceable spare, jack and wheel spanner**, and a fire extinguisher. Not strictly demanded of light vehicles, but you are heading somewhere with no tow trucks.

Buy the triangles and vests as a set before you leave South Africa. They cost less than the fine and far less than the negotiation.

Speaking of which: pay official fines only, ask for a written receipt, keep it polite and keep it slow. Drivers who carry the right kit and the right papers rarely have a problem. Drivers who are missing one item and in a hurry are the ones who end up paying for it.

## The drive: Komatipoort to Pomene, leg by leg

From the border to the lodge is roughly **690 km**. Call it 11 hours of driving on a good day, before you add the border itself, fuel stops, lunch and the sand at the end.

**Leg 1 — Ressano Garcia to Maputo, ~100 km, about 1½ hours.** The EN4 is a well-maintained toll road. Carry **meticais in cash** for the plazas; the amounts are small but a card is not reliable. Draw cash at the border or in Maputo rather than assuming an ATM later.

**Leg 2 — Maputo to Maxixe/Inhambane, ~450 km, 6 to 7 hours.** North on the EN1. Long, flat, fast in places, with towns, speed limits that drop suddenly, and traffic policing that takes those limits seriously. Fuel is available along the way.

**Leg 3 — Maxixe to the Pomene turnoff, ~140 km.** The turnoff is roughly **8 km north of Massinga** if you're coming up from the south. **Fill up before this point** — Inhambane or Maxixe is the sensible choice, because supply this far up can be patchy and there is no fuel at Pomene at all. Massinga is the last town of any size, about 50 km from the lodge, and it's where the big shop happens for a self-catering stay.

**Leg 4 — the last ~50 km.** Dirt, then deep soft sand into the Pomene reserve. This is where a normal car stops being an option and a high-clearance **4×4 becomes essential**. Deflate before the sand, keep momentum, and read our [honest guide to the drive and the tyre pressures](/journal/drive-to-pomene-4x4-guide) before you attempt it for the first time.

### Where to break the journey

Do not try this in one day. The maths almost works on paper and never works in practice — you will hit the sand at dusk, on the one section where being tired is genuinely dangerous.

Two sensible splits:

1. **Cross early, overnight around Inhambane or Maxixe**, then a short, relaxed morning run into Pomene with time for the supply shop. This is the one we'd suggest.
2. **Cross late, overnight in Maputo**, then a long second day. Workable, but it puts you on the sand in the afternoon and leaves no margin if the EN1 is slow.

Either way, **arrive in daylight**. That rule has no exceptions here.

## Conditions to check before you leave

Southern and central Mozambique flooded badly between December 2025 and February 2026, and the EN1 was cut in several places. The Inhambane coastal corridor has been operating normally since around the middle of 2026 — the beaches, the lodges and the reef are all fine — but **some EN1 stretches and secondary roads are still being repaired**. Conditions change with each rainy season.

So check the route close to your departure date rather than trusting a guide written months earlier, this one included. The South African border authorities publish operating updates, and the [Lebombo port of entry contact details](https://www.dha.gov.za/index.php/contact-us/land-ports/88-mpumalanga-ports-of-entry/637-lebombo-port-of-entry) are on the Department of Home Affairs site if you want to confirm hours directly before a holiday crossing.

One more thing that isn't about the road: **Inhambane province is a malaria area**. Speak to a travel clinic about prophylaxis well before you go, especially for children, where dosing is by weight and several common drugs are unsuitable. We're not going to give you a dose in a blog post — that conversation belongs with a nurse who can look at your family.

The genuinely large numbers on this trip are fuel and the days themselves. The border is cheap. It's only expensive when you arrive without a document and lose a day, or a trip.

## Arriving at Pomene

The reward for all of this is a place that the difficulty keeps quiet. Pomene sits between the open Indian Ocean and a wide tidal estuary, inside a reserve, at the end of that sand track — no town, no resort strip, no street lighting.

[**Iz-Ma-An Lodge**](/#lodge) is on the dune above the beach: four en-suite bedrooms across two units, sleeping eight, solar powered, about a hundred metres of sand between the balcony and the water. It's **self-catering** by design, which is why the Massinga shop matters and why the [self-catering versus all-inclusive comparison](/journal/self-catering-vs-all-inclusive-mozambique) is worth reading before you plan your food for the week — half-board and fully inclusive options exist if you'd rather not cook, with two weeks' notice.
